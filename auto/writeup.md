### Writeup:

When tasked with making a PID controller, I was already very comfortable with the concept from my previous robots (https://github.com/patribots4738) but was eager to finally work with the equations from scratch, rather than relying on a battle tested library. It’s those kinds of practices that lead you to fully understand the background of a concept rather than just being able to explain it to someone.

Since the PID math was very well documented and straightforward, I added an aerodynamic drag calculation, to try to make the plot feel more realistic. I noticed that since we are controlling velocity via acceleration, once we get to our desired speed, we can just turn the acceleration off and guarentee to never overshoot. It felt too much like working with a bang bang controller. I wanted a little bit more realism in the simulator, without having to crank K_I to bring forceful instability.

Most of my learning came from working with NumPy and Matplotlib together. I had previously done this kind of work in MATLAB directly, and my Python/JS charting experience was with other libraries (uPlot for the win). Since I am taking ECE 141 right now and learning about the greater details of feedback controllers, I wanted to mark important response times on the plot of velocity over time, which included my 10-90% rise time, +- 1% settling time, and max overshoot percentage/time. NumPy let me store the simulation samples in arrays and find where the velocity crossed the rise thresholds or entered the 1% settling band fairly easily, which I had expected for it since that’s basically its whole purpose. 

With Matplotlib, I learned how to combine curves, use a second axis for drag force, add annotations, and connect text-box inputs to plot updates so I could “tune” the PID gains without redeploying the code. Presenting the response visually helped me explain what the gains changed while still being able to see the bigger picture in ways that waiting for code to redeploy doesn’t often get you. Sliders are technically a better way to do this, but I like being unconstrained. 

I do think it is important to note that while the drag curve showed how a calculated drag force increased with speed, I didn’t apply it to the vehicle's motion. Sort of just ran out of time on that one.

All in all, I enjoyed using Matplotlib, but I must say that the interface felt surprisingly slow when moving the window or typing into the text boxes. I wonder if its a product of using one thread, or maybe if matplotlib was just not made for that, because I cross referenced it with some other friends and they also had the same issue with moving and resizing the window. At least it’s not prevelant to the project itself. I’ll leave the full investigation of that for the future, perhaps multithreading will resolve those sorts of problems.
